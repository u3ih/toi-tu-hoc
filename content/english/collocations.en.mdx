---
title: Collocations
description: Why I stopped learning one word at a time and started learning chunks.
---

There was a phase where my vocabulary was decent, my grammar was not wrong, and my sentences still
came out slightly *off*. Readers understood them, but nobody says it that way.

Something I once wrote: *"I want to make a decision about this problem"* — that one is fine. But I
also wrote *"do a decision"*, *"big rain"*, *"powerful coffee"*. All three are grammatical and all
three are wrong.

It should be **make a decision**, **heavy rain**, **strong coffee**. No rule explains why. That is
just what people say.

## English is assembled from chunks, not words

This is an idea I got from Michael Lewis, who wrote *The Lexical Approach* in 1993. Cambridge has
[a free summary](https://www.cambridge.org/elt/blog/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Learning-Language-in-Chunks.pdf)
of chunk-based learning, quicker than reading the whole book.

His point: native speakers do not build sentences from grammar plus vocabulary. They **pull out whole
chunks** already stored in their heads.

*"I'd like to make a reservation"* is not assembled on the spot. It comes out as one block.

That explained two things I had never understood:

- Why native speakers talk fast without thinking — they are retrieving, not calculating.
- Why I knew many words and still spoke slowly — I was assembling one brick at a time.

## Four kinds of chunk worth noticing

| Kind | Example | Why collect it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collocations | *heavy rain*, *make a decision*, *strong accent* | Not predictable from the individual words |
| Phrasal verbs | *put up with*, *run into*, *come across* | The whole means something other than its parts |
| Fixed expressions | *to be honest*, *as far as I know*, *the thing is* | Usable immediately, almost impossible to get wrong |
| Sentence frames | *It's not that ..., it's just that ...* | One frame serves hundreds of ideas |

The fourth kind is the highest return. Learn one frame, use it forever.

## How I changed my cards

My Anki card used to be: one word, one meaning.

Now, for each new word, I also collect **3 to 5 chunks** it habitually appears in.

For *decision*:

- make a decision
- reach a decision
- a tough decision
- reverse a decision
- the decision to *(do something)*

Those five lines taught me far more than "decision = quyết định". And more importantly: they taught me
exactly the places where I would otherwise have mistranslated from Vietnamese.

<Callout type="tip" title="Cloze cards">
The front is a real sentence with exactly one gap:

*They had to ____ a difficult decision.*

I don't have to recall what *decision* means. I have to recall **which word goes with it**. That is
the thing I was actually missing.
</Callout>

## How I check whether a chunk is real

When I'm not sure people say something, I don't guess. I look it up.

| Tool | For | Address |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collocation dictionary | Which verbs and adjectives a noun takes | [ozdic.com](https://ozdic.com) |
| SkELL | Real corpus sentences, plus frequent partners | [skell.sketchengine.eu](https://skell.sketchengine.eu) |
| YouGlish | Hear real people say the exact phrase on video | [youglish.com](https://youglish.com) |
| Google in quotes | Compare result counts between two phrasings | — |

The Google method: search `"make a decision"`, then `"do a decision"`, and compare the counts. A
difference of thousands of times settles it. Crude but fast, and it is the one I use most.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't trust one-way translation">
The most dangerous chunks are the ones that translate smoothly out of your own language.

*"Mình rất thích nó"* → I used to write *"I very like it"*. Correct by Vietnamese logic, completely
wrong in English.

*"Mở đèn"* → *"open the light"*. It has to be *turn on the light*.

These are not grammar errors, so a grammar book cannot fix them. Only meeting them often and
collecting them can.
</Callout>

<Callout type="story">
I discovered this when I asked a native speaker to read a paragraph I had written.

They corrected not a single grammar mistake. But they underlined seven places and wrote beside them:
*"we'd say it like this"*. All seven were collocations.

I took exactly those seven into Anki. It was the most useful set of cards I have ever had, and it
came from a passage under 200 words.
</Callout>

## Where to find chunks

You don't need a separate source. They are already sitting in what you listen to and read — your eyes
used to skate over them because they are made of familiar words.

Once I started paying attention, I changed the question I ask while reading. It used to be *"which
word don't I know?"*. Now there is a second one: *"which phrase do I understand instantly but would
never have produced myself?"*

The second question yields far more valuable cards than the first.
