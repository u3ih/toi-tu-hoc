title: Comprehensible input
description: How I pick material at the right difficulty, and how I make it easier when it isn't.
---

## The original idea

There is one concept I think explains almost everything: **comprehensible input**.

It is material where you get the main idea, even without understanding every word.

Both extremes are useless:

- Material you understand nothing of is just noise. It does not matter how much of it you consume.
- Material you understand 100% of teaches you nothing new.

The useful part sits in between.

There is a number behind this idea:
[Hu and Nation (2000)](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2000-Hu-Density-and-comprehension.pdf)
found that to read comfortably without a dictionary, you need to know roughly **98% of the words** on
the page — about 6 unknown words per page of a novel. I go into it in
[How many words is enough](/english/how-many-words/).

## The difficulty scale I use

| Comprehension | What it means | What I do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Below 50% | Too hard | Switch material. Don't push through. |
| 50–70% | Hard, tiring | Only for intensive reading of a short passage |
| 70–90% | **The golden zone** | Most of my time goes here |
| Above 95% | Too easy | Good for keeping the habit, but no progress |

<Callout type="story">
For the first six months I forced myself to watch shows made for native speakers, because I assumed
harder material meant faster learning.

I understood about 30%. Every session was exhausting and I remembered nothing.

When I dropped down to learner material — which felt a bit childish to me at the time — things
finally started moving. A few months later I came back to that same show and understood over half of
it.
</Callout>

## Making material easier yourself

You don't always have to change material. You can lower the difficulty of the thing you actually
like.

<Steps>
### 1. Know the content in advance
Read a plot summary in your own language first. Knowing what is about to happen frees your brain up
to process the language.

### 2. Watch it a second time
The second pass through the same episode is always far easier than the first pass through a new one.
I think this is the most underrated trick there is.

### 3. Read first, then listen
Read the subtitles or transcript first, then listen again without looking at the text.

### 4. Slow it down
0.85x genuinely helps. But treat it like training wheels — temporary, not permanent.
</Steps>

<Callout type="tip" title="Signs you are in the right zone">
You can follow the thread of the story and you are curious about what happens next, but the odd
sentence still slips past you.

If you have to rewind constantly to follow along, the material is too hard — however much you like
it.
</Callout>
