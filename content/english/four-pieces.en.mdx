---
title: The four pieces
description: The one frame I use to check whether my study week is missing something.
---

Of everything I have read about language learning, this is the frame I found most useful. It comes
from Paul Nation, a researcher in New Zealand, who calls it the **four strands**. The original paper
is [free to read here](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2007-Four-strands.pdf), 12 pages long.

The idea: a decent study programme needs all four of the things below, and each should take roughly
**a quarter** of the time.

I like it because it does not tell you what to study. It just gives you a way to check: *which piece
did I leave empty last week?*

## What the four pieces are

| Piece | What it means | What I actually do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Meaning-focused input** | Listening and reading material you mostly understand | Watch shows, listen to podcasts, read fiction |
| **2. Meaning-focused output** | Speaking and writing to convey a real idea, not to do an exercise | Journal, summarise an episode, have a conversation |
| **3. Language-focused learning** | Studying the language directly: words, grammar, pronunciation | Anki, a grammar book, sound drills |
| **4. Fluency development** | Redoing what is already easy, but faster | Skim an easy book, replay an old episode, retell one story several times |

The condition on piece 1 is specific: the material has to be at a level where you already know about
**98% of the words** — a number from
[Hu and Nation (2000)](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2000-Hu-Density-and-comprehension.pdf).
I go into it in [How many words is enough](/english/how-many-words/).

Piece 4 is one I had never heard anyone mention before, and the one I neglected longest.

## The piece I forgot: fluency development

Piece 4 teaches you nothing new. That is exactly the point.

It takes what you **already know** and makes using it fast and automatic. Without this piece you
become someone who knows a lot of words and still speaks haltingly — because every word takes a
second to retrieve.

Three things I do for it:

- **Easy reading, fast.** Pick a book where I barely need a lookup, then read for 15 straight minutes,
  stopping for nothing.
- **Replay old episodes.** An episode I have already seen twice, listened to a third time at 1.25x.
- **Tell it three times.** Pick a story, tell it in English in 4 minutes. Then tell the same story in
  3 minutes. Then 2. The content doesn't change, only the time you have.

<Callout type="tip" title="The 4–3–2 exercise">
That third one is a named exercise in the research, called 4/3/2. Nation describes it in
[*Improving speaking fluency* (1989)](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/1989-Fluency.pdf).

The first time you think while you talk. By the third, the sentences come out ready-made. I find it
far more effective than telling three different stories in the same nine minutes.
</Callout>

## How I actually split my time

Nation says the four should be equal. I never managed that, and I don't think I should pretend
otherwise.

My first year leaned hard into piece 1:

| Stage | Input | Output | Language-focused | Fluency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Year 1 | ~70% | ~5% | ~20% | ~5% |
| Year 2 | ~55% | ~15% | ~15% | ~15% |
| Year 3 onwards | ~40% | ~30% | ~10% | ~20% |

I still think leaning into input early is right — with nothing in your head, there is nothing to
produce. But "leaning" is not the same as "zero".

<Callout type="warning" title="A piece at zero is the real problem">
The real mistake is not an uneven split. It is leaving one piece at zero for months on end.

I left output at zero for nearly two years. By the time I needed to speak, I understood everything
being said to me and could not assemble a decent sentence myself.
</Callout>

## The weekend self-check

Every Sunday I spend about two minutes on this:

<Steps>
### 1. How many minutes did I listen and read this week?
Under 5 hours and nothing else matters as much as pulling that number up.

### 2. Did I speak or write a single sentence of my own?
Three journal sentences count. Zero is the problem.

### 3. Did I have any session studying the language directly?
Anki, looking one unfamiliar structure up properly, drilling a sound.

### 4. Did I have any session doing something easy, at speed?
This is the piece I forget most. If every session in a week felt hard, this is usually what is
missing.
</Steps>

<Callout type="story">
I once totalled up a month and found: 42 hours of listening, over 900 Anki reviews, and **not one
sentence of English written**. In a whole month.

On paper that month looks diligent. But if anyone had asked me to write an English email, I would
still have stared at the screen for ten minutes.

That is when I added one line to my tracker: *how many English sentences did I create this week?*
</Callout>
