---
title: How many words is enough
description: The numbers that told me where I stood, and which words are worth the effort.
---

When I started, I kept asking "how many words do I need to read a book?". Nobody could answer, and I
had no way to answer it myself.

Later I found the research on **lexical coverage** — how much of a text's vocabulary you need to know
in order to understand it. The numbers below changed how I choose material and how I choose Anki
cards.

## The most important number: 98%

[Hu and Nation (2000)](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2000-Hu-Density-and-comprehension.pdf)
found that to **read comfortably without a dictionary**, you need to know about **98%** of the words on
the page. At 95% you can still read, but it is tiring and you are guessing a lot.

Those two numbers sound close together. Counted another way, they are not:

| Coverage | One unknown word every | On a ~300-word page | How it feels |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 90% | 10 words | ~30 unknown | Unreadable |
| 95% | 20 words | ~15 unknown | Readable but exhausting |
| 98% | 50 words | ~6 unknown | Flows, mostly guessable |
| 99% | 100 words | ~3 unknown | Like your first language |

This is the reasoning behind the [70–90% golden zone](/english/comprehensible-input/) I mention
elsewhere. Understanding 70–90% of the *meaning* usually corresponds to knowing about 95–98% of the
*words*.

## How many words each mark takes

Here the counting unit is a **word family**, not individual words. *Nation, national, nationality,
international* count as one family, because knowing the root lets you infer the rest.

| Word families | Covers about | What you can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1,000 | 75–80% | Get the gist of very simple conversation |
| 2,000 | ~90% | Read graded readers |
| 3,000 | ~95% | Read everyday material, with effort |
| 6,000–7,000 | ~98% *(listening)* | Follow films and podcasts without much strain |
| 8,000–9,000 | ~98% *(reading)* | Read novels and news without lookups |

The last two rows come from [*How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening?* (Nation,
2006)](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf).

The most-used list of common words today — the 2,800-word [NGSL](http://vli-journal.org/issues/03.2/vli.v03.2.browne.pdf) —
covers over **92%** of ordinary English text on its own.

<Callout type="info" title="Why listening needs fewer words than reading">
Spoken language draws on a considerably narrower vocabulary than written language.

But don't celebrate too early: listening is hard not because of the words, but because sounds link and
drop. I go into it in [When you know the words but can't hear them](/english/listening-gap/).
</Callout>

## What these numbers changed for me

**One, I stopped blaming myself for not managing novels.** 8,000 word families is a long road. Not
reading them in your first year is normal, not a personal failing.

**Two, I measure material before committing to it.** Open a page at random, count the unknown words.
More than 15 on a page and I shelve the book for six months. It takes a minute and saves me weeks of
frustration.

**Three, I pick Anki cards by frequency.** This was the biggest change.

<Callout type="tip" title="My card-making rule">
When I meet an unfamiliar word, I ask two questions:

1. **Is it inside the ~5,000 most common words?** If yes → make a card now.
2. **If not, how many times have I met it?** First time → skip it, move on. Second or third time →
   now make the card.

A rare word you met once will be forgotten after you learn it, because nothing will remind you of it.
</Callout>

## How many words a year

[Nation estimates](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1059712.pdf) that in a classroom setting, about
**1,000 word families a year** is good going.

That is a discouraging number. But it is a classroom number — roughly five words per hour of class.

Self-study through heavy listening and reading is considerably faster, because you meet a word in many
different contexts across a day rather than in two lesson periods. I have no figures of my own to
offer, but I am sure of one thing: **the words you gain per year scale almost linearly with your
contact hours.**

That is also why I stopped hunting for vocabulary tricks. No trick makes up for the hours.

## Estimating your current vocabulary

A few free tools I have used:

| Tool | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| [testyourvocab.com](https://testyourvocab.com) | An estimate of total vocabulary, 5 minutes |
| [lextutor.ca](https://www.lextutor.ca) | Paste a passage in, see what frequency band it uses |
| Anki statistics | Cards you have learned — the truest number, since you made each one |

Don't run these weekly. Their margin of error is larger than a week of your progress. I do it about
**every six months**.

<Callout type="story">
The first time I took a vocabulary test, it came out around 4,000. I was quite proud — until I checked
the table above and saw that 4,000 is still short of comfortable reading.

That was when I understood why novels felt so tiring even though I "knew almost every word". The
problem was not how much I knew, but that **6 unknown words per page** and **15 unknown words per
page** are completely different experiences.

I put the novels down and went back to easier fiction for another year. It was the right call.
</Callout>
