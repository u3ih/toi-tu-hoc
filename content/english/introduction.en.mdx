I'm Hiếu, a developer like any other. It's just that for a long time I've wanted to write a blog — to share things, to
keep notes on what I figure out, or simply to have somewhere to practise new tech.

With AI as strong as it is now, putting this site together really only took a few prompts, so I built it and I'm still
filling in the content. The first thing I want to share is how I've been learning English.

I'm not good at English yet, and I'm still working on it. But I've tried a lot of methods and got plenty of them wrong,
so maybe what I went through can help someone.

It took me a while to see the actual problem: **I had been studying English like a subject on an exam, not like a
language.**

## Where I went wrong

Looking back, there were three mistakes. I think a lot of people could make the same ones.

**One: learning words in isolation.** I copied new words into a notebook, one per line, with the Vietnamese meaning next
to it, and tried to memorise them. I got hundreds of them down — but when someone said one out loud, it went past me
like wind past my ear. All I really knew was what the word looked like. I'd heard it a few times, but at normal speaking
speed I couldn't catch it.

**Two: trying to go too deep on grammar.** Breaking down the passive voice, third conditionals, reduced relative
clauses. But in real speech a sentence is gone in a moment; there's no time to analyse anything. Studying that hard and
seeing nothing improve is what made it so easy to give up.

**Three: forcing myself to speak too early.** I went to English speaking practice while my vocabulary was still very
thin, because the internet told me this was the best way to improve. I could talk, but I talked wrong — and nobody was
there to correct it. Repeat a mistake often enough and it becomes a habit.

<Callout type="story">
  Now and then I'd sit and listen to two native speakers having an ordinary conversation — on the street, on YouTube,
  wherever. I'd realise I knew almost every word they were using. But strung together, at normal speed, I couldn't
  process any of it. I'd put the hours in, but I had no idea how many more would be enough. The old way of studying
  didn't suit me anymore.
</Callout>

## What actually changed things

I looked into an approach called **immersion** — spending time soaked in the language instead of studying it from the
outside.

The idea is simple: instead of learning _about_ English, I use English. The first thing I did was switch every device I
own to English — and I've kept it that way for nearly ten years. Whenever I get the chance I try to listen to and read
real material — films, podcasts, stories — the same things native speakers consume. I'm not sure whether this counts as
lucky or unlucky, but being a dev means I read a lot of English documentation either way.

At first I understood very little, and it was discouraging. I kept reading anyway. After running into the same things
enough times, some sentences just clicked — ones I had never sat down and memorised.

That's how people learn languages. It just took me a long time to find that out.

## What's in the posts that follow?

This is my own record: what I did, in what order, with which tools, and where I got stuck.

I wrote them for the version of me from years ago — and for you too, whoever is reading this.

<Callout type="warning" title="Who this is not for">
  If you need an IELTS score in two months, don't follow me. Immersion gives results that last, but the first few months
  feel like nothing is happening. Pushing a test score up quickly is mostly exam technique, and the methods below won't
  fit that goal very well.
</Callout>
