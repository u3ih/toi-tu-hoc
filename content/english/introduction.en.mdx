I started learning English in sixth grade. By the time I had a job, I still couldn't get through a
single episode of anything without Vietnamese subtitles.

That bothered me for years. I wasn't lazy. I took extra classes, bought the books, installed the
apps. The results barely moved.

It took me a long time to see the actual problem: **I had been studying English like a subject on an
exam, not like a language.**

## Where I went wrong

Looking back, there were three mistakes. I think a lot of Vietnamese learners make exactly the same
three.

**One: learning words in isolation.** I copied new words into a notebook, one per line, with the
Vietnamese meaning next to it. I memorised hundreds of them. But when someone actually said one out
loud, I didn't recognise it — I only knew what it looked like on paper, never what it sounded like
inside a real sentence.

**Two: going too deep on grammar.** I could break down the passive voice, third conditionals,
reduced relative clauses. But in real speech a sentence is gone in a second. There is no time to
analyse anything.

**Three: forcing myself to speak too early.** I joined an English club while my vocabulary was still
very thin. I could talk, but I talked wrong. And because I repeated the mistakes often enough, they
became habits. Undoing them later was exhausting.

<Callout type="story">
I once sat near two native speakers having an ordinary conversation in a café. I realised I knew
almost every single word they were using. But put together, at real speed, I understood nothing.
That was the moment I knew my old approach had run out of road.
</Callout>

## What actually changed things

I switched to an approach called **immersion** — spending time inside the language instead of
studying it from the outside.

The idea is simple: instead of learning *about* English, I use English. Every day I listen to and
read real material — films, podcasts, stories — the same things native speakers consume.

At first I understood very little. I kept going anyway. And after a few months, sentences started
clicking into place that I had never sat down and memorised.

That is how people learn languages. Nobody told me that while I was in school.

## What these notes are

This is my own record: what I did, in what order, with which tools, and where I got stuck.

I wrote them for the version of me from ten years ago.

<Callout type="warning" title="Who this is not for">
If you need an IELTS score in two months, don't follow me. Immersion gives results that last, but
the first few months feel like nothing is happening. Exams need test technique, which is a different
problem.
</Callout>
