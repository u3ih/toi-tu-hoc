Almost nothing on this site is my own idea. I only tested them on myself, kept what worked, and
dropped what didn't.

This is where they came from. I have noted **what I actually took** from each source, because a bare
list of links is available everywhere.

## The original ideas

| Source | What I took from it |
| --- | --- |
| [Stephen Krashen](https://www.sdkrashen.com) — books free to read on his own site: [*Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition*](https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf) and [*The Power of Reading*](https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/the_power_of_reading.pdf) | The comprehensible input idea: the language that gets in is the language you understand, not the language you memorise. The second book is why I started reading fiction instead of textbooks. |
| [Paul Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications) — nearly all of his papers free to download, including a Vietnamese translation of *What do you need to know to learn a foreign language?* | [The four pieces](/english/four-pieces/) and every number in [How many words is enough](/english/how-many-words/). The most practical source I found. |
| Michael Lewis — *The Lexical Approach* (1993). Free summary: Cambridge's [*Learning language in chunks*](https://www.cambridge.org/elt/blog/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Learning-Language-in-Chunks.pdf) | The idea that English is assembled from **ready-made chunks**, not grammar plus vocabulary. Leads straight into [Collocations](/english/collocations/). |
| [Refold](https://refold.la/roadmap/) — an immersion roadmap, free to read | A clear division into stages, and the "don't speak early" idea. I did not follow all of it, but it did more than anything else to reduce my confusion in year one. |
| [Antimoon](https://www.antimoon.com) — an old site, ugly interface, real content | The idea of collecting whole sentences instead of isolated words. The two people who wrote it taught themselves English to near-native level and documented it very frankly. |

<Callout type="warning" title="I don't follow any source completely">
There is a part of every source above that I dropped.

Krashen almost entirely rejects deliberate study — I found Anki and a few weeks of grammar genuinely
useful. Refold has rules I find too rigid.

If you follow a system to the point of not daring to deviate, you are probably optimising for the
system rather than for yourself.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Which parts are research and which are mine">
Any number with a citation is research, and you can check it.

Any number without one — hours I studied, cards I deleted, pages I read — is my own experience. It is
useful for getting a sense of scale, but it is not evidence of anything.
</Callout>

## Word lists and corpora

| Source | For |
| --- | --- |
| [NGSL](https://www.newgeneralservicelist.com) | The 2,800 most common words, free download. I use it to decide which words deserve a card. |
| [Lextutor](https://www.lextutor.ca) | Paste in a passage, see what frequency band it sits in. The fastest way to tell whether a book is too hard for you. |
| [COCA](https://www.english-corpora.org/coca/) | An American English corpus. Check whether a phrasing is genuinely common. |

## Dictionaries

I go into how to choose in [Monolingual dictionaries](/english/monolingual-dictionary/). These are the
specific ones:

- [Oxford Learner's Dictionaries](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com) — definitions written
  with a limited vocabulary. The first one to switch to.
- [Merriam-Webster Learner's](https://learnersdictionary.com) — similar, American-leaning, with more
  example sentences.
- [Longman](https://www.ldoceonline.com) — marks which words are in the most common bands, handy for
  telling whether a word is worth learning.
- [ozdic](https://ozdic.com) — a collocation dictionary. Small, ugly, extremely useful.
- [SkELL](https://skell.sketchengine.eu) — no definitions, just real corpus sentences.
- [Forvo](https://forvo.com) — hear real people from different regions say a word.
- [YouGlish](https://youglish.com) — hear a word or phrase across hundreds of real videos.

## Listening and reading sources

My principle is still [pick few, go deep](/english/resources/). This list is for choosing **one**, not
for bookmarking all of it.

**For beginners:**

- [BBC Learning English](https://www.bbc.co.uk/learningenglish) — a graded path, with a transcript for
  every piece.
- [VOA Learning English](https://learningenglish.voanews.com) — news read slowly, with a vocabulary
  capped at about 1,500 words. Transcripts included.
- [ELLLO](https://elllo.org) — thousands of short conversations, many accents, transcripts, sorted by
  level. This is what I use for [dictation](/english/listening-gap/).

**Reading:**

- [Extensive Reading Foundation](https://erfoundation.org/wordpress/) — graded reader lists by level.
  The safest way in.
- [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org) — free out-of-copyright books. Note that most are
  old, with harder prose than everyday English.

**Intermediate and up:** anything you genuinely want to watch. The only requirement is accurate
English subtitles, so you can go back over what you missed.

## Tools for mining material

| Tool | Role |
| --- | --- |
| [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) | Spaced repetition. Free on every platform except iPhone. |
| [FSRS setup guide](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) | Anki's newer algorithm: fewer reviews for the same retention. Enable it in deck options. |
| [Ddict](/english/ddict-setup/) | Look words up on the page you are reading. The tool I use most. |
| [Readlang](https://readlang.com) | Read the web and books in the browser, click a word for its meaning, save it. |
| [asbplayer](https://github.com/killergerbah/asbplayer) | Sync subtitles to video, clip both the sentence and the audio into Anki. Free and open source. |
| [Language Reactor](https://languagereactor.com) | Dual subtitles and in-place lookups on streaming platforms. The free version is enough. |
| [Tatoeba](https://tatoeba.org) | A bank of example sentences with translations; find real sentences containing the word you are learning. |

## Finding people to correct you and speak with

- [italki](https://www.italki.com) — paid tutors by the session; prices vary enormously by teacher.
- Language exchange apps — free, in return for teaching your own language. Quality varies wildly.
- Learner communities on forums — a good place to ask someone to look over a short piece of writing.

<Callout type="story">
In my first three years I must have bookmarked over a hundred links. I had a folder called "English"
with ten subfolders inside it.

I opened that folder again later and counted: I had genuinely used **six** of them.

The list above is longer than six, because different things suit different people. But if you bookmark
this whole page and close the tab, you have just repeated my exact mistake.

Pick one listening source. Open it. Today.
</Callout>
