This list is deliberately short.

My problem early on was not a shortage of resources. It was the opposite: I saved far too many things
and never used any of them properly.

<Callout type="warning" title="Pick few, go deep">
Pick **one** listening source and **one** reading source for this month. Only change when the old one
has become too easy for you.

It took me about a year to learn this.
</Callout>

## Tools

| Tool | What I use it for |
| --- | --- |
| [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) | Reviewing the sentences I collected myself |
| [Ddict](/english/ddict-setup/) | Looking words up on the page while reading the web |
| [asbplayer](https://github.com/killergerbah/asbplayer) | Syncing subtitles to video: replay the exact sentence you missed, and clip the audio |

These three are everything I use regularly. I have tried dozens of other apps and dropped them all.

## Listening

**Beginner** — material made for learners, slow-speech podcasts, video channels explaining things in
simple English with visuals.

The three free sources I used most at this stage:
[BBC Learning English](https://www.bbc.co.uk/learningenglish) (graded by level, every piece has a
transcript), [VOA Learning English](https://learningenglish.voanews.com) (news read slowly, with a
vocabulary capped at about 1,500 words), and [ELLLO](https://elllo.org) (short conversations, many
accents, transcripts included).

**Intermediate** — interview podcasts, long-running animated series, explainer videos on a topic you
already know well in your own language.

**Advanced** — anything native speakers watch. I favour material with a lot of natural conversation
over scripted narration, because real life sounds more like the former.

## Reading

- **Graded readers** — vocabulary capped by design. This is the safest way in. There are lists by
  level at the [Extensive Reading Foundation](https://erfoundation.org/wordpress/).
- **Long web serials** — nearly endless, everyday language, free. The downside is uneven prose.
- **LitRPG and progression fiction** — this genre repeats a lot of vocabulary about stats, skills and
  combat. All that repetition makes it easier to get into than I expected.
- **News and non-fiction** — useful, but a narrower vocabulary than fiction.

<Callout type="story">
I tried to start with a literary classic, assuming a "serious" book would teach me more.

I quit after two chapters, and it took another six months before I dared pick up an English book
again.

The thing that actually got me into a reading habit was a long web serial that, honestly, is not
well written. But I read more than two thousand pages of it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Not sure whether a book is within reach?">
Paste a passage into [Lextutor](https://www.lextutor.ca) to see what frequency band its vocabulary
sits in. Or do it the crude way: open a page at random and count the unfamiliar words. More than 15
per page is too hard — I explain that number in
[How many words is enough](/english/how-many-words/).
</Callout>

## Community

Self-study does not mean studying alone.

I'd suggest finding a group to ask when you are stuck and, more importantly, to see other people
struggling the same way. There were stretches where the only thing that kept me going was knowing
that plateaus are normal.

## A fuller list

This page only lists what I use regularly. If you want everything I drew ideas from — books,
