## The first thing I realised

An adult native speaker knows roughly **20,000 to 35,000 words**. That is before counting the phrases
they habitually go with and the small differences in usage. This number and the milestones below come
from [Paul Nation's research](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf).

Learning that number was discouraging. But it also killed an illusion: **there is no trick that
memorises all of that.**

The only thing that produces that volume is **time in contact with the language**. Every technique in
these pages exists for one purpose: to make that contact time more effective and easier to sustain.

The less discouraging part: you don't need 20,000 words to use English. About 8,000 word families
lets you read novels without a dictionary, and 3,000 covers most everyday material — I gave that
number its own piece: [How many words is enough](/english/how-many-words/).

## Learning to not understand everything

This was my hardest habit to break.

Early on, an unfamiliar word meant stopping to look it up immediately. Twenty lookups on a single
page. Forty minutes for that page — and afterwards I couldn't tell you what it said.

Then I changed approach.

<Callout type="tip" title="My 80% rule">
If I get about 80% of the passage's meaning, I keep reading. No lookup.

The remaining words work themselves out after a few dozen encounters elsewhere. And they genuinely do.
</Callout>

## Learning top-down

School teaches bottom-up: pronunciation, then vocabulary, then grammar, then finally putting
sentences together.

Immersion runs the other way. I met **complete material** from day one — a whole episode, a whole
chapter — and only then pulled small pieces out to study closely.

It sounds strange, but it is exactly how children learn their first language. They hear whole
sentences for a long time before they can say anything.

## A realistic route

These are the stages I went through. The timings are estimates — some people are faster, some slower.

| Stage | What I did | How I knew I'd moved on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–3 months | Basic grammar + listening to learner material | Understand learner material by ear |
| 3–12 months | Daily listening and reading + Anki | Read simple stories without constant lookups |
| 1–2 years | Switch to an English–English dictionary, watch native material | Follow most of a film without subtitles |
| 2 years onwards | Start speaking and writing seriously | Speak fluidly, and catch my own mistakes |

## How much grammar I actually learned

I did it **once, quickly, only to the level of recognition**.

The goal was never to get exercises right. The goal was that when I meet an unfamiliar structure
while reading, I don't stall on it.

Concretely:

- About 6 weeks on one basic grammar book.
- I did not finish the exercises. Read the explanation, look at the examples, move on.
- I only went back to look something up when I met an unfamiliar structure while reading. Never
  studied ahead just in case.

## The four things I do daily

<Steps>
### 1. Active listening — 20 to 40 minutes
Nothing else. No phone. This is the part that moves me fastest.

### 2. Reading — 20 to 30 minutes
With [Ddict](/english/ddict-setup/) giving me one-second lookups, I don't lose momentum.

### 3. Anki review — 10 to 15 minutes
Old cards only. I don't create new cards during a review session — that makes the session run forever.

### 4. Background listening — whenever
Commuting, cooking, cleaning. Far less effective than active listening, but costs almost no extra
time.
</Steps>

<Callout type="story">
In month one I set a target of two hours a day. I lasted four days.

Then I dropped to thirty minutes. It felt like very little, but I kept it up for four straight months.
Looking back, thirty minutes a day for four months is far more than two hours a day for four days.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="How I check my routine for gaps">
The four items above lean heavily on listening and reading. They were enough for the first year, but
later I needed a way to see what my week was missing.

The frame I use is [the four pieces](/english/four-pieces/) — two minutes every Sunday, and it is
what showed me that for two years I had barely produced a single English sentence of my own.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't optimise too early">
I once spent almost three weeks picking apps, picking decks, reading method comparisons. Those three
weeks taught me exactly zero.

One mediocre hour of study beats three hours of researching how to study.
</Callout>
