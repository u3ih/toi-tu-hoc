## I spoke too early

When I started, I assumed that speaking well meant speaking a lot. That sounds reasonable.

So I joined an English club while my vocabulary was still very thin. I could talk — but only by
translating each Vietnamese sentence into English in my head.

A few months of that and the wrong pattern became a reflex. Later, when my level had risen, the same
wrong sentences still came out of my mouth, and undoing them was exhausting.

<Callout type="warning" title="A mistake repeated often enough becomes a reflex">
Unlearning a reflex costs far more than forming the right one in the first place.
</Callout>

## So when should you start

My answer: **when you can comfortably understand native material by ear.**

For me that landed at somewhere past 400 hours of listening. It will differ for everyone.

You *can* speak at any point; nobody is stopping you. But the quality of what comes out is capped by
how much you have taken in. Speaking early does not make you improve faster.

## Pronunciation is the exception — start it early

This is the one exception. I recommend starting pronunciation **from day one**.

The reason: if you cannot produce a sound, your ear struggles to pick it out. Pronunciation practice
pays back into listening.

<Steps>
### 1. Get familiar with the IPA chart
No need to memorise it. You just need to see a symbol in a dictionary and know how to say it.

### 2. Find the sounds you can't currently tell apart
Vietnamese speakers commonly struggle with: final consonants (usually swallowed), /θ/ and /ð/ in
*think* and *this*, /s/ and /ʃ/ in *sea* and *she*, and long vs. short vowels like *ship* and *sheep*.

### 3. Shadowing
Listen to a sentence, repeat it immediately. Copy the rhythm and intonation, not just the individual
sounds.

What I used to get wrong was trying to pronounce each word correctly — that is reading aloud, not
shadowing. The two-stage approach I use now is in
[When you know the words but can't hear them](/english/listening-gap/#shadowing-doing-it-right).

### 4. Record yourself and listen back
Hearing your own voice is unpleasant. But without a teacher, it is the only honest feedback you get.
</Steps>

## Practising alone

It sounds odd, but this is what I did most, and it worked better than I expected. It also costs
nothing.

- Narrate what you are doing, in English, in your head or under your breath.
- After an episode, summarise it in five sentences.
- Argue with yourself about a topic you care about.

## Finding someone to speak with

When you are ready, there are three options. I have used all three.

| Option | Upside | Downside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A partner at your level | Comfortable, no fear of mistakes | Easy to reinforce each other's errors |
| Native speakers on a language-exchange app | Free, real accents | Quality varies wildly |
| A paid tutor | Systematic correction | Costs money |

On a tight budget, I would put the money into a few tutor sessions a month, right at the point where
you start speaking. That is when feedback is worth the most.
