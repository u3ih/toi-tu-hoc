## Why I stopped learning word lists

I used to keep a notebook of new words. One word per line, with the Vietnamese meaning beside it.

The problem showed up when I met those same words in real sentences. I knew *concern* meant "mối
quan tâm". Then I hit **"To whom it may concern"** and was lost.

A word carries several meanings depending on context, attaches to particular prepositions, and holds
nuance that a short translation cannot express.

So I switched: **learn whole sentences, not isolated words.**

Later I took it one step further: for each word, I also collect three to five phrases it habitually
appears in. That has its own piece: [Collocations](/english/collocations/).

## How I make cards

The process is simple. While listening or reading, I hit a sentence where **exactly one thing is
unknown to me**. I take that whole sentence as a card.

<Callout type="tip" title="One unknown per card">
This is the most important rule I learned.

A sentence with three unknown words does not teach you three words. It teaches you none of them,
because you have no context left to infer any of them from.
</Callout>

### What one of my cards contains

- **Front:** the whole sentence, with the target word in bold.
- **Back:** the meaning of that word. In [English](/english/monolingual-dictionary/) if possible. Plus
  audio if available.
- Never several words crammed into one card.

I use [Ddict](/english/ddict-setup/) to look things up on the page, then copy the sentence and meaning
into Anki. Ddict cannot push cards into Anki, so I do this by hand. It sounds tedious, but retyping
the sentence is itself one more repetition.

## The Anki settings I use

| Setting | My value | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New cards per day | 10 | Enough to progress, not enough to drown |
| Maximum reviews per day | 200 | Stops a skipped day from exploding |
| Algorithm | [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html#fsrs) | Fewer reviews for the same retention |
| Learn ahead | Off | Avoids cards piling into the end of the day |

## Three rules I got from nearly quitting

**1. Review every day, including lazy days.** I skipped three days and the due count doubled. Seeing
that number killed my motivation outright. On a genuinely busy day, five minutes is fine — as long as
I open it.

**2. Delete cards without mercy.** A card I have failed three times in a row is a broken card, not
proof that I am slow. Usually the sentence is too long, or it has two unknowns. I delete it or rewrite
it.

**3. Anki serves listening and reading; it does not replace them.** If a week goes by where I spent
more time on cards than on listening and reading, I know I am heading the wrong way.

<Callout type="story">
The second time I nearly quit Anki, my deck had passed 3000 cards and reviews were taking almost an
hour a day.

I did something that felt like a waste at the time: I deleted about 800 cards I had never once
remembered.

Reviews dropped to twenty minutes. And I kept going for another two years. If I hadn't deleted them
that day, I would have quit entirely.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Which words deserve a card">
Not every unfamiliar word does. I filter with two questions: is this word inside the ~5,000 most
common words, and how many times have I now met it?

The reasoning behind that filter is in [How many words is enough](/english/how-many-words/).
</Callout>

<Callout type="danger" title="About shared decks you download">
Studying someone else's cards works far worse than your own, because you have no original context —
you don't remember where you met the sentence.

The only exception I find reasonable is a ~2000-word foundation deck for the first few months.
</Callout>
