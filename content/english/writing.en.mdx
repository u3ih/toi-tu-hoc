title: Writing in English
description: Why I practised writing before speaking, and the three-step loop I use.
---

I neglected writing for almost two years. The reasoning was simple: my goal was to *speak*, so
writing was secondary.

Looking back, that was a bad calculation. Writing is the cheapest and least frightening way to start
producing English of your own.

## Why write before you speak

**You have time.** Speech has to come out in a second. Writing lets you think, revise, look things
up. The first sentence you ever produce should not be one you produce under pressure.

**You can see your mistakes.** Speech evaporates. Writing leaves the error sitting there, rereadable
and countable.

**It forces active recall.** Listening and reading only need recognition. Writing makes you pull words
out of your own head, and that is when you find out which words you actually have.

**And it transfers straight to speaking.** The sentences I wrote over and over about myself, my
interests, my job — later, when speaking, they came out ready-made.

## Start with three sentences

Start small enough that there is no excuse to skip it.

Three sentences a day. About what you just watched, just ate, or were just annoyed by. They do not
have to be good.

Prompts I use when stuck:

- Summarise the episode you just watched in 5 sentences.
- Three things you did today, one sentence each.
- One thing you disagree with, and why.
- Explain your job to someone outside your field.

<Callout type="tip" title="Write from ideas, not from Vietnamese sentences">
This is my most important rule for writing.

If you compose a Vietnamese sentence and translate it, you get sentences like *"I very like it"*.

What I do instead: hold only the **idea**, then ask *"how did people in that show say this?"*. If no
structure comes to mind, write a simpler sentence. A simple correct sentence always beats a complex
translated one.
</Callout>

## The three-step loop

Writing with nobody correcting you just reinforces your own errors. This is the loop I use.

<Steps>
### 1. Write, no lookups
Write straight through. Where you don't know a word, leave a gap or drop in your own language and
keep moving.

This step measures what you actually have available in your head.

### 2. Reread and self-correct the next day
Leave it a day before editing. Edit immediately and your eyes still see what you *meant* to write,
not what you wrote.

Now you can use a dictionary, check [collocations](/english/collocations/), and revise freely.

### 3. Get it corrected, then take the errors to Anki
Have someone correct it. Only collect the **repeated** errors, not one-offs.

An error that shows up three times in a month deserves a card. A single stumble does not.
</Steps>

Step 3 is the decisive one. Without it, the first two steps are just writing for fun.

## Who corrects you

| Option | Upside | Downside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Online language-learning communities | Free, real people | Slow, uneven quality |
| A language model | Instant, unlimited | Tends to over-polish; you have to ask well |
| A paid tutor | Systematic, remembers your past errors | Costs money |

A tip for machine correction: don't ask *"fix this paragraph"*. It will rewrite it into something far
better than you could produce and you will learn very little.

I ask like this:

> This is written by an English learner. Only correct what is **wrong** or what **a native speaker
> would not say**. Keep the voice and the difficulty level. For each correction, add one short line
> explaining it. Do not rewrite the paragraph.

That last sentence is the important one.

## Where Vietnamese speakers commonly slip

These are the errors that took me longest to fix, and I see almost everyone make them.

- **Articles.** Vietnamese has no *a/an/the*, so I either dropped them entirely or sprinkled them at
  random. This was the last error I fixed, and mostly through reading volume rather than learning
  rules.
- **Plurals.** *many book*, *two child*. Vietnamese nouns don't change form.
- **Tense.** In Vietnamese, time lives in *đã, đang, sẽ* — drop them and the sentence still works. In
  English the verb has to change.
- **Dummy subjects.** *"Hôm nay trời mưa"* has no subject. English requires one: *It's raining today*.
- **Adjective order.** *a red big car* sounds wrong to a native speaker; it should be *a big red car*.
  Nobody memorises this order, but read enough and it starts to grate.

<Callout type="story">
I wrote a three-sentence journal every day for six months. It was tedious, and some weeks I managed it
exactly three times.

But when I reread the whole thing from the start, I counted the same article error more than 40 times
in the first three months, and almost gone by month six.

I don't remember a single day where I "studied" articles. It resolved itself because I got it wrong
enough times and saw myself getting it wrong enough times.
</Callout>

## Volume or care

I split it in two, the same way I split [the two modes of reading](/english/reading/).

**Volume writing** — the three-sentence journal, comments online, notes. No editing, no lookups. The
goal is to loosen up and get faster.

**Careful writing** — one ~200-word paragraph a week, run through the full three-step loop above. The
goal is to find your repeated errors.

Volume without any careful session and your errors freeze exactly where they are.
