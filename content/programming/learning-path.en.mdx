description: The path I took, and how I knew when to move on to the next step.
---

## The rule I came away with

Learn just enough to **build one thing that runs**, then go back and dig deeper.

I tried it the other way round: finish the theory first, build later. I quit in month four, with
nothing to show for it.

## The order I suggest

| Stage | What to learn | When you can move on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The syntax of one language + a few basic algorithms | You can write a ~100-line program on your own |
| 2 | Git, the command line, how to read documentation | A strange error stops making you panic |
| 3 | One real project, start to finish | You have something that runs for other people |
| 4 | Tests, design, optimisation | Someone else can change your code |

The column that matters is the right-hand one. Don't change stage on a schedule — change on a signal.

<Callout type="warning" title="The tutorial trap">
Watching a tutorial and typing along feels almost exactly like understanding. That feeling
disappears the moment you sit in front of an empty file.

How I got out of it: after each tutorial, rebuild **a different variant** of it without looking back.
Change the requirements a little. The part where you get stuck and have to work it out yourself is
the part where the learning happens.
</Callout>

<Callout type="story">
My first real project was a small, ugly tool that only I used. It saved its data to a text file,
because at the time I didn't know how to use a database.

It taught me more than the entire year of tutorials before it. Because for the first time, nobody was
telling me what the next step was.
</Callout>
